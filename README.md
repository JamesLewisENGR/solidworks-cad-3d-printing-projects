# SolidWorks CAD & 3D Printing Projects

This repository contains a selection of projects I have completed while developing my SolidWorks CAD and 3D-printing skills. I have been using SolidWorks to practice part modeling, assemblies, engineering drawings, and design techniques while also preparing for the CSWA certification.

For many of these projects, I take the design beyond the CAD model by exporting the part, preparing it for 3D printing, and producing a physical version. This lets me compare the model to the actual printed part and learn how dimensions, tolerances, orientation, supports, and other design decisions affect the final result.

## What I Am Practicing

- SolidWorks part modeling
- Sketching and fully defining sketches
- Extrudes, cuts, fillets, chamfers, and other features
- Assemblies and mates
- Engineering drawings
- Design intent and dimensioning
- CSWA practice problems
- Designing parts for 3D printing
- Exporting CAD models for additive manufacturing
- Print orientation and support considerations
- Fit and tolerance testing
- Design iteration based on physical prints

## Projects

### Adjustable Lamp — SolidWorks Assembly & 3D Printing

I designed a multi-part adjustable lamp in SolidWorks with articulated arm sections, pivot joints, a base/clamp, and a separate lamp-head assembly. I created the individual parts, assembled and mated the components, established the adjustable motion, and created engineering drawings for the project.

The CAD and drawing stage is complete, and the next step is 3D printing the components and testing the physical assembly. I will document fit, movement, tolerances, and any design changes that come from the physical prototype.

### SolidWorks to 3D Print — Tolerance & Fit Testing

I created a simple tolerance test in SolidWorks to see how small differences in CAD clearance translate to the fit of actual 3D-printed parts. After printing the test pieces, I physically compared the fits and used the results to better understand how much clearance I need when designing mating components.

In this test, a **0.05 mm clearance produced a very tight fit**, while **0.10 mm was snug but still movable**. The results gave me a practical reference for future parts while also showing why physical testing matters when moving from CAD to additive manufacturing.

## Design-to-Print Process

For projects that I 3D print, my general process is:

1. Create and fully define the design in SolidWorks.
2. Check dimensions, geometry, and intended fit.
3. Export the model for 3D printing.
4. Prepare the part in the slicer and determine print orientation and supports when needed.
5. Print the physical part.
6. Check the finished part for fit, dimensions, and function.
7. Modify the CAD model and reprint when changes are needed.
